Why Eden Is Important — A Foundational Paragraph

Eden Cemetery is not simply a burial ground — it is the accumulated memory of Black America's most audacious act of self-determination.

Since Black Philadelphians began establishing their own sacred spaces in the 1790s — churches, beneficial societies, and cemeteries that offered dignity where the dominant culture offered none — they were building toward Eden.

Established in 1902 by Black founders who refused to accept that death would be another site of dispossession, Eden became the guardian of what other institutions could not hold. When Lebanon Cemetery and Olive Cemetery — home to Philadelphia's Black citizens — were forced to close and their residents reinterred, Eden absorbed them all.

What this means is that Eden now holds the complete record of Black Philadelphia's founding generation: the abolitionists who operated the Underground Railroad in plain sight, the ministers who built the first Black churches, the physicians who founded the first African American graduate medical programs, the poets who told America it could not be free while it held people in chains, the architects who designed the buildings of elite American institutions, and the musicians who carried the sorrow and joy of a people to the world's great stages.

Philadelphia was the civic laboratory of Black America — the city where Black people invented how to protest, how to claim rights, and how to build institutions that could sustain a community under conditions of profound oppression — and the people who did that are buried at Eden.

When you walk Eden's 53 acres, you are not visiting a cemetery. You are time-traveling through the foundation of Black American identity, standing on ground that holds 93,000 ordinary people who did extraordinary things — in a city that taught the world what freedom really means.

Ten Elevator Pitches

Each pitch offers a distinct angle. Phrases in italics are lifted directly from workshop participants.

01The Founding Pitch

Philadelphia was the civic laboratory of Black America. The first HBCU. The first Black churches. The first beneficial societies. The first abolitionist networks that put the cracks in the foundation of American slavery. The men and women who built those institutions — who taught the world how to protest, how to claim civil rights, how to build a nation within a nation — are buried at Eden Cemetery. You cannot do Black Philly history without Eden Cemetery. And you cannot understand America without Black Philly.

02The F.U.B.U. Pitch

In 1902, a group of Black Philadelphians did something radical: they built their own. For generations, Black people had been denied dignity in death — buried in unmarked graves, in segregated sections, in cemeteries they did not control. So Black Philadelphia built Eden. For You. By You. Ninety-three thousand souls rest on 53 acres of Black-owned, Black-controlled, sacred ground. That's ours. It has been ours from the beginning. It still is.

03The Underground Railroad Pitch

William Still — the father of the Underground Railroad, the man who documented every freedom seeker he helped escape, who kept the records that became the foundation of African American genealogy — is buried at Eden. He is one of 93,000. Eden is a designated National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom site and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The freedom movement didn't end with emancipation. The proof is right here — seeing what is no longer, and what must never be forgotten.

04The "Hi Oprah" Pitch (lifted directly from workshop)

"Hi Oprah. A serious historical injustice needs to be rectified. Eden Cemetery's significance has been strategically undermined — intentionally — and it has caused broken trust and lost community connections to Black American history, from the ordinary to the extraordinary, for over 124 years." Eden is where Stedman Graham's family rests. It is where Black Philadelphia rests. It needs a champion. We're asking you to be one.

05The Time-Travel Pitch

What if you could sit in the same space as Marian Anderson, Frances Watkins Harper, and William Still? Eden Cemetery is time travel — the past in the present. It is where 93,000 stories of ordinary people who did extraordinary things are waiting to be heard. Stories = History. Past = Now. The result of what was is the continuation of what we are. Come sit with your ancestors. History ignored is history repeated. Eden won't let you forget.

06The Civic Democracy Pitch

The Declaration of Independence was signed in Philadelphia. But it was Black Philadelphians who forced America to live up to it — who challenged its hypocrisy from the very beginning, in their churches, their courts, their newspapers, and their streets. What they practiced in Philadelphia, the world eventually practiced. Philly is at the center of a national conversation about how American history should be remembered. The architects of that tradition — from the ordinary to the extraordinary, for over 124 years — are buried at Eden. This is not a cemetery. It is the birthplace of American democracy's conscience.

07The Sacred Space Pitch

Since the 1790s, Black Philadelphians have protected their right to honor their dead — in dignity, in community, in reverence. In a time when enslaved people had no control over their bodies in life or in death, having a headstone, having a grave someone could visit, having ancestors who had come to a place of rest — that was resistance. That was love. Eden is the culmination of over 200 years of that tradition. A safe space. A sacred space. A third space. Come honor them. Come sit in the beauty. Come see what is no longer — and feel what endures.

08The Reinterment / Justice Pitch

Lebanon Cemetery. Olive Cemetery. The great Black burial grounds of Philadelphia — home to some of the most important Black Americans in history — were forced to close. Forced. Their residents were reinterred at Eden. Other cemeteries were not forced to move. Only theirs. Even in death, the mistreatment continued. But Eden held them. Eden holds them still. What looks like a cemetery is actually an act of resistance — 124 years of refusing to let Black history be buried twice.

09The America's-Oldest-Black-Cemetery Pitch

Eden Cemetery is a representation of the importance of the identity of Black life — and one of the oldest continuously operating Black-founded cemeteries in the United States. Its 93,000 residents include an Olympic gold medalist, the composer of gospel's greatest hymns, a U.S. Congressman, the architect of elite American universities, the abolitionist who documented the Underground Railroad, and one of America's greatest singers. This is not a local story. A representation of Black history — for half of America's history — has persisted here. Eden is America.

10The Future Pitch

We need Eden not just because of what happened in the past — but because of what is happening right now. Black spaces need support now more than ever. Eden is how we transport the past to our future. It is the place where we learn from the people who built Black America — who practiced Afrofuturism before the word existed, who imagined a future they might not live to see and built it anyway. We need resources to continue informing future generations about our history. We need Eden to survive — so the stories it holds can teach us ways to improve our now.
