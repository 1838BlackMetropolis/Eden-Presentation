Walking Tour · Guide Script · Full Tour, 90–120 minutes

Learning Black History Through Burial Grounds

A walking tour of Eden. Eight stops, from the first burial in 1902 to the present.
PREPARED BY 1838 BLACK METROPOLIS  ·  RECLAIM. REWRITE. & RESTORE.

The question this tour asks

Eden was founded on June 20, 1902, when the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania chartered the Eden Cemetery Company and Black civic leaders purchased roughly 53 acres in Collingdale. It was created partly in response to segregation and to the displacement of Black burial grounds in Philadelphia. It holds both relocated burials and people buried directly at Eden, and its plot names are themselves a commemoration of Black history. Every stop on this walk returns to one question.

How does Eden continue the tradition of preserving generations of Black life, Black Community Building, and Black Institution Building?

How each stop is told

The same six beats at every plot. The stops differ in length and in the people they center, but the structure never changes. That is what lets a guide keep the walk coherent across eight sections and two centuries.

  • How the plot received its name. What or whom it commemorates; whether it was named for a person, a displaced cemetery, an institution, or a historical figure; when it was established or in use, if known; and whether the namesake is actually buried there.
  • Introduce a few people buried there. Connect their history to Eden through death and burial. Example sequence: “[Name], who passed [date], was originally buried at [place]. Their remains were [transferred to Eden with that cemetery / buried directly at Eden / moved separately to Eden / later removed from Eden].” Then explain their legacy.
  • Connect that person to someone at another stop. “[Name] worked with [person], whom we will encounter later in the [plot] section,” or “This takes us back to [person], whom we discussed in the [plot] section.” Gesture toward the earlier or upcoming plot or headstone whenever it is visible.
  • Additional detail. Add one or two people who deepen the central person’s network or extend the chronology.
  • If time permits. Offer a lesser-known person; a theme connecting everyone, such as medicine, education, religion, art, or fraternal life; or a larger historical issue, such as body-snatching, cemetery displacement, scientific racism, or commemoration.
  • Transition. End by explaining why the group is moving to the next plot.

Three tiers of content. Every line in this script sits in one of them.

  • Confirmed. Ready to say to the public.
  • Optional detail. Confirmed, but expendable if time is short.
  • Research note. Promising but not ready for public interpretation. Do not say it on the tour.

StartSpringfield Road entrance

3–5 minutes. Welcome the group to Eden. Explain the length, the terrain, and the basic route.

Guide script. Eden was founded on June 20, 1902, when the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania chartered the Eden Cemetery Company. Black civic leaders purchased approximately 53 acres of land here in Collingdale to establish it. Eden was created partly in response to segregation and the displacement of Black burial grounds. It contains both relocated burials and people buried directly at Eden, and its plot names serve as a commemoration of Black history.

Set the question. “The key question we will think about is: How does Eden continue the tradition of preserving generations of Black life, Black Community Building, and Black Institution Building?”

01Celestine

7–10 minutes. Chronological span 1902–1976. Central theme: Eden’s first burial and white resistance. Central person: Celestine Moseley Cromwell. Direct Eden burial.

About this plot. The Celestine section was named for Celestine Moseley Cromwell, Eden Cemetery’s first burial. She was the wife of Willis H. Cromwell, one of the twenty-nine members of the Eden Cemetery Company’s advisory board.

Optional detail. Celestine Reserve No. 1 and Celestine Reserve No. 2 are later expansions of the original section. The date of Reserve No. 1 is not documented in the National Register nomination; Reserve No. 2 dates to 1976.

Her death and burial. Celestine Moseley Cromwell died on August 8, 1902. Eden planned to bury her on August 12, but white Collingdale residents and borough officials attempted to prevent the funeral procession from entering the new Black cemetery. Her remains were held for approximately thirty-six hours before she was finally buried at Eden on August 14, 1902.

Who had the authority to decide where Black bodies could be buried, and whether those places would remain protected?

Walking transition, Celestine to Lehmann (5–8 minutes). Introduce the section-name categories and the cemetery’s geography as you walk.

In early America, free and enslaved Black Philadelphians were frequently buried in public, marginal, segregated, or poorly marked spaces. Black communities had limited legal control over burial land, which left graves vulnerable to disturbance and erasure. This was a form of oppression African Americans dealt with throughout their history.

To counter it, in the 1790s and 1800s independent Black churches established churchyards and burial arrangements. Church burial grounds were among the earliest spaces over which Black Philadelphians exercised institutional and spiritual control. Mutual-aid organizations also helped Black communities assume collective responsibility for death and burial. One of the plots we will see is named for Richard Allen, who in 1787, alongside Absalom Jones and others, established the Free African Society, which provided mutual aid, care for the sick, and burial assistance.

02Richard Allen

7–9 minutes. Chronological span 1787 to the present. Central theme: religious institution-building and control over burial. Main connection: Richard Allen to Absalom Jones to Lebanon.

About this plot. The Richard Allen section is named in honor of Richard Allen, founder and first bishop of the African Methodist Episcopal Church. The plot commemorates him, but his remains are not here. Say that distinction right after the plot name: “Richard Allen gives this section its name, but he is not buried here. His remains are entombed at Mother Bethel AME Church in Philadelphia.”

Legacy. In 1787, Allen, Absalom Jones, and other Black Philadelphians established the Free African Society. It provided mutual aid, assistance to the sick, and support surrounding death and burial. In 1810, Mother Bethel acquired the Bethel Burying Ground. Thousands of Black Philadelphians were buried there through the nineteenth century. Mother Bethel later sold the property, and the site eventually became part of Weccacoe Playground, while thousands of remains apparently remained beneath the transformed landscape.

Jones’s and Allen’s passing. Allen’s collaborator Absalom Jones died in 1818. Jones’s remains were moved several times, including from Lebanon Cemetery to Eden. “When we reach the Lebanon section, we will follow that complicated burial journey.” Richard Allen died on March 26, 1831. He was buried at Mother Bethel AME Church, where his remains are now held in a sarcophagus, a stone coffin above ground, in the church’s lower-level museum.

Transition to Lehmann. “Allen and Jones created institutions to care for Black Philadelphians when white institutions would not. More than a century later, Martin Lehmann and Eden’s other founders confronted the same fundamental problem on the scale of an entire cemetery.”

03Lehmann

6–8 minutes. Chronological span 1859–1924. Central theme: building and administering Eden.

About this plot. The Lehmann section is named for Martin J. Lehmann (1859–1924), a cigar maker who served as treasurer of the Eden Cemetery Company. Lehmann was one of Eden’s five charter members, whom Eden identifies collectively as its founders.

What Lehmann represents. Not merely a person for whom a plot was named, but the financial and administrative work required to create a Black-controlled cemetery: obtaining a charter; acquiring land; defending the cemetery legally; selling and recording plots; arranging burials and transfers; and sustaining the cemetery as an institution.

Research note. The National Register nomination confirms that the section was named for Lehmann but does not establish its exact naming date. It classifies Lehmann as a “newer” section and indicates that it was being used substantially by 1958. It also cautions that a section’s naming may have occurred after burials began there. Do not say that Lehmann is buried in the Lehmann section until his exact grave location is confirmed. The documented point is that the section was named for him.

04Lebanon

15–20 minutes. Central themes: cemetery displacement, reinterment, civil-rights networks, Black control over burial. Principal nodes: James Forten and William Still. Optional clusters: Absalom Jones; Catto and LeCount; the Still-Anderson family; Black medicine.

First, gather and orient the group. [Position the group where visitors can see the Lebanon sign and as much of the section as possible. Allow everyone to settle before beginning.]

We are now standing at the Lebanon section, one of the most historically important sections at Eden. Lebanon is not named for a famous individual. It preserves the name of an entire Black cemetery that no longer exists. [Pause and gesture across the section.] The people buried here did not all arrive in the same way. Some were removed from Lebanon Cemetery in Philadelphia and transferred here in 1903. Others were buried directly at Eden years or decades later. That distinction, between a transferred ancestor and a later Eden burial, will help us understand this section.

Second, how Lebanon received its name. Lebanon Cemetery was chartered in Philadelphia on January 24, 1849. It stood near what is now Nineteenth Street and Snyder Avenue in South Philadelphia. It was a nonsectarian cemetery created for African Americans who were excluded from many white cemeteries. Its establishment represented progress: Black Philadelphians had secured a dedicated place in which members of their community could be buried. But ownership and designation did not guarantee permanent protection.

In 1882, newspapers exposed an organized body-snatching operation at Lebanon. Bodies had been removed from graves and sold to Jefferson Medical College for anatomical study. In 1899, Philadelphia condemned Lebanon Cemetery. As the city expanded, the land occupied by the cemetery became valuable for other uses. When Eden opened, thousands of remains were removed from Lebanon and brought here. By 1903, the original cemetery had closed.

The name “Lebanon” does more than identify this section. It preserves the name of a Black place that was erased from Philadelphia’s physical landscape.

Third, Absalom Jones: a burial journey spanning four places. [Gesture back toward the Richard Allen section.] At the Richard Allen section, we discussed the Free African Society, which Richard Allen, Absalom Jones, and others established in 1787. Absalom Jones died on February 13, 1818.

Remains now elsewhere. He was first buried in the churchyard of the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas, the church he helped establish with assistance from James Forten and other members of Philadelphia’s Black community. When that church moved, Jones’s remains were transferred to Lebanon Cemetery. When Lebanon closed, his remains were transferred again, from Lebanon to Eden. In 1991, Jones’s remains were removed from Eden and returned to the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas. Jones’s journey demonstrates that reinterment was not always a single event. Some Black remains traveled through several burial landscapes as churches moved, cemeteries closed, and cities changed.

Fourth, James Forten: the central nineteenth-century node. Another person connecting Jones to this section is James Forten. James Forten died on March 4, 1842.

Transferred. Forten was originally buried at the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas at Fifth and Adelphi Streets. His remains were subsequently moved to Lebanon Cemetery and eventually transferred here to Eden. [Gesture toward Forten’s marker or in its general direction.] Forten was born in Philadelphia in 1766. As a young man, he served aboard an American privateer during the Revolutionary War. He later became a successful sailmaker, employer, abolitionist, and supporter of Black education. He is especially valuable to this tour because he connects several generations and several sections of Eden.

  • Connection backward to Absalom Jones. Forten helped Absalom Jones establish the African Episcopal Church of St. Thomas in 1794. He also supported schools for Black children and adults. Religious independence, education, mutual aid, and burial were not separate projects. They were different parts of building an independent Black community.
  • Optional: connection forward to William Whipper at Olive. [Gesture toward Olive if visible; otherwise in the direction the tour will travel.] Later, at the Olive section, we will meet William Whipper. Forten and Whipper participated in the American Moral Reform Society, concerned with abolition, education, temperance, economic advancement, and collective responsibility. Forten died in 1842 and Whipper in 1876. They belonged to overlapping generations: Forten helped create institutions that Whipper’s generation entered, expanded, and sometimes challenged.
  • Optional: connection within Lebanon to James J. G. Bias. James Joshua Gould Bias died on July 15, 1860. Transferred. Bias was originally buried at Lebanon Cemetery and was transferred to Eden when the cemetery was displaced. He was a physician, minister, abolitionist, Vigilance Committee leader, and participant in the Colored Conventions movement. Convention records place Bias, Whipper, and Stephen Smith within the same larger movement of Black political organization.

Memory cue. Jones helped build religious independence. Forten connected religious, educational, and abolitionist work. Bias joined medicine to abolition and political organizing. Whipper and Smith connected reform to business and economic power.

Fifth, Octavius Catto: murder, voting, and reinterment. [Shift the group’s attention or move a few steps if Catto’s location can be indicated.] Octavius Valentine Catto died on October 10, 1871.

Transferred. He was originally buried at Lebanon Cemetery. His remains were transferred to Eden on May 14, 1903. Catto was only thirty-two when he was murdered during election-day racial violence in Philadelphia. His work brought together education, voting rights, Black military service, streetcar desegregation, baseball, and public commemoration.

  • Connection to William Still. Catto worked within the same Philadelphia civil-rights network as William Still. Both participated in the campaign to desegregate the city’s streetcars.
  • Connection to Caroline LeCount. Catto was engaged to educator and activist Caroline LeCount. LeCount died on January 24, 1923. Direct Eden burial. Unlike Catto, LeCount was not transferred here with Lebanon Cemetery. She died twenty years after the transfer and was buried directly at Eden in the section where Catto had been reinterred. This is one of the clearest examples of the distinction we are following. Catto was moved here because Lebanon Cemetery was closed. LeCount was later buried here after Lebanon had become an Eden section. Their presence in the same section reunites two connected lives, but their bodies arrived through different historical processes.
  • Optional memory cue. Catto represents displacement. LeCount represents return, or reunion.

Sixth, William Still: the generational bridge. William Still died on July 14, 1902, about one month before Celestine Cromwell’s burial opened Eden.

Sequence unconfirmed. Still is recorded as buried at Eden, but the precise sequence between his death and interment should be confirmed in Eden’s first interment register before the guide states that Eden was unquestionably his first burial location. Until the record is checked, do not label Still as transferred or as a direct burial in the spoken tour. Say only that he is buried here.

Still was an abolitionist, Underground Railroad organizer, businessman, writer, and historian. His 1872 book, The Underground Rail Road, preserved accounts of hundreds of freedom seekers and the people who assisted them. He connects multiple sections of the tour.

  • Backward to James Forten. Still belonged to a later generation of Philadelphia abolitionists. Forten’s generation built many of the religious, educational, and reform institutions through which Still’s generation worked.
  • To John Brown, back or forward depending on route. Still worked with Frances Ellen Watkins Harper in Philadelphia’s abolitionist community. Harper is buried in the John Brown section.
  • To Catto and LeCount. Still, Catto, and LeCount participated in the struggle to desegregate Philadelphia streetcars.
  • To the Still-Anderson family. Still also connects abolition to the emergence of Black-controlled medical and social-service institutions. His daughter, Caroline Still Anderson, died on June 1, 1919. Direct Eden burial. She was buried directly at Eden in the Lebanon section. Caroline was a physician and educator. Her husband, Matthew Anderson, was a Presbyterian minister and the founder of Berean Presbyterian Church. [Gesture back toward Celestine.] Matthew Anderson is buried in Celestine, which we encountered at the beginning of the tour. Together, Caroline and Matthew developed medical, educational, religious, and social-service programs through Berean.

The Still-Anderson family connects two plots and two generations. William Still represents abolition and the Underground Railroad. Caroline Still Anderson represents medicine and education. Matthew Anderson represents religion and community institution-building.

Optional deep dive: the Black medical network. [Use only if approximately four or more minutes remain.] The Lebanon section contains a legacy of Black medical and public-health history.

  • Caroline Still Anderson died on June 1, 1919. She was one of the earliest Black women physicians in Philadelphia.
  • Nathan Francis Mossell died on October 27, 1946. Direct Eden burial. He was buried at Eden long after the Lebanon transfer. Mossell became the first Black graduate of the University of Pennsylvania’s medical school and founded Frederick Douglass Memorial Hospital, which offered care to Black patients while creating professional opportunities for Black physicians and nurses excluded from many white institutions.
  • Gertrude Bustill Mossell died on January 21, 1948. Direct Eden burial. She was a journalist, author, educator, suffragist, and fundraiser, and helped generate support for the hospital her husband founded. Together, the Mossells show that institution-building required more than a physician. It required writing, fundraising, public persuasion, women’s organizing, and community trust.
  • Connection back to Celestine. Rebecca J. Cole, buried in Celestine, also belonged to Philadelphia’s pioneering generation of Black women physicians. The evidence establishes a shared professional world, but not every proposed personal relationship among Cole, Anderson, and Mossell has been confirmed. Describe them as an overlapping medical network, not automatically as close friends or collaborators.

Optional deep dive: Laura Wheeler Waring. Laura Wheeler Waring died on February 3, 1948. Direct Eden burial. She was a later direct burial at Eden, not a person transferred from the original Lebanon Cemetery. Waring was a painter and educator known especially for her portraits of prominent Black Americans. She gives the guide two connections. [Gesture toward Olive.] Jessie Redmon Fauset, whom we will meet at Olive, was Waring’s friend and traveling companion; they traveled together in Europe and North Africa. [Gesture back toward Celestine.] Waring also painted Marian Anderson, who is buried in Celestine. Waring at Lebanon, Fauset at Olive, Anderson at Celestine: three sections, one friendship and one portrait.

Lebanon summary. [Bring the group’s attention back to the whole plot.] Lebanon contains several different kinds of burial history. Absalom Jones’s remains passed through this section and were later returned to St. Thomas. James Forten, James Bias, and Octavius Catto were transferred after earlier burial grounds closed. Caroline LeCount, Caroline Still Anderson, the Mossells, and Laura Wheeler Waring were buried at Eden later, after Lebanon had become one of its established sections. The plot therefore represents both displacement and continuity. The dead moved here from a place that disappeared. Later generations then chose, or were given, burial among them.

Transition out of Lebanon. “Lebanon preserves the name of a lost Black cemetery. Our next stop shows another way a plot name can communicate memory: it could express allegiance to a historical figure and the unfinished struggle that figure represented.” [Move toward John Brown.]

05John Brown

Main node: Frances Ellen Watkins Harper. Chronological anchor: February 22, 1911. Direct Eden burial.

Open with the distinction. “John Brown gives this section its name, but he is not buried here.” Then introduce Harper: “Frances Ellen Watkins Harper died on February 22, 1911, and was buried directly at Eden in the John Brown section.”

Why the location matters. Her burial location carried political meaning. Harper regarded Brown as a friend and hero. She was also close to William Still through Philadelphia’s abolitionist and Underground Railroad networks, which connects this stop back to Lebanon.

Optional cluster. The United States Colored Troops, and the use of cemetery space to preserve Civil War memory.

06Bowers

About this plot. This section was named for Henrietta Bowers Duterte, identified as America’s first Black woman undertaker, not for her brother John C. Bowers.

Optional detail. “A sign reading only ‘Bowers’ does not tell us which Bowers is being commemorated. Eden’s National Register documentation identifies Henrietta Bowers Duterte, a pioneering undertaker, business owner, abolitionist, and philanthropist.”

John C. Bowers. Died in 1873. Transferred. He was originally buried at Lebanon Cemetery and was later transferred to Eden.

07Olive

13–18 minutes. Central themes: a displaced cemetery, intergenerational inheritance, reform, literature, folklore, and religion. Principal figures: William Whipper and Jessie Redmon Fauset. Optional figures: Stephen Smith, Arthur Huff Fauset, Joseph Brister. Central comparison: Whipper was transferred with Olive; Fauset was buried later in Eden’s Olive section.

First, gather and orient the group. [Stop at the Olive sign. Let the group face the section before speaking.] Like Lebanon, Olive preserves the name of an earlier African American cemetery in Philadelphia. It is important to distinguish between Olive Cemetery, the original Philadelphia burial ground, and Olive, the section here at Eden. The original cemetery dates to 1849. The remaining burials were transferred to Eden in 1923. So 1923 is not the year Olive Cemetery was established. It is the year its dead were brought here.

Second, how Olive received its name. Olive Cemetery stood in West Philadelphia near Forty-fourth Street and Girard Avenue. It was associated with Black abolitionist, businessman, minister, and philanthropist Stephen Smith. Smith used wealth generated through business to support Black institutions, including the Philadelphia Home for Aged and Infirm Colored Persons. The Home maintained its own burial ground nearby. The dead from the Stephen Smith Home burial ground were brought to Eden in 1903. The remaining burials from Olive Cemetery followed in 1923. [Gesture over the section.] As at Lebanon, some people in this section were transferred with the old cemetery. Others died later and were buried directly in the Eden section.

Third, William Whipper: transferred with Olive. William Whipper died on March 9, 1876. Transferred. He was originally buried at Olive Cemetery. His remains were transferred to Eden when the old cemetery was removed. Whipper was an entrepreneur, abolitionist, moral reformer, convention activist, and Underground Railroad participant.

  • Connection back to James Forten at Lebanon. [Gesture back toward Lebanon.] At Lebanon, we met James Forten. Forten and Whipper participated in the American Moral Reform Society, which joined abolition to questions about education, economic life, temperance, print culture, and collective responsibility. Forten was born in 1766, Whipper in 1804. Their work overlapped, but they also represent successive generations of Black reform.
  • Connection to Stephen Smith. Whipper’s strongest professional and personal connection was Stephen Smith. Smith and Whipper became partners in a highly successful lumber and coal business. Their business interests helped support their antislavery activities, including Underground Railroad work. The relationship was also familial: Whipper married Smith’s sister, Harriet. Smith and Whipper show that Black economic history and abolitionist history cannot be separated neatly. Their businesses created money, transportation networks, property, and relationships that could be mobilized for antislavery work.

Memory cue. Forten helped establish the reform network. Smith supplied economic and institutional power. Whipper connected business, reform, and transportation.

Fourth, Jessie Redmon Fauset. Now we move forward eighty-five years. Jessie Redmon Fauset died on April 30, 1961. Direct Eden burial. Unlike Whipper, Fauset was not moved here from the original Olive Cemetery. She died decades after the transfer and was buried directly in Eden’s Olive section. Whipper’s remains came here because a cemetery was displaced. Fauset came here after Olive had become part of Eden’s commemorative landscape.

Fauset’s legacy. Fauset was a novelist, poet, teacher, translator, and literary editor. As literary editor of the NAACP’s The Crisis, she helped publish and encourage writers associated with what became known as the Harlem Renaissance. Her presence extends Olive’s story from nineteenth-century abolition and moral reform into twentieth-century literature, education, and Black cultural production. She also helps us see historical inheritance. Fauset did not personally continue Whipper’s lumber business or abolitionist work. But she inherited a world shaped by generations of Black institution-building, education, organizing, and print culture.

  • Connection back to Laura Wheeler Waring at Lebanon. [Gesture back toward Lebanon.] At Lebanon, we encountered painter Laura Wheeler Waring. Fauset and Waring were friends. They traveled together in Europe and North Africa, including a documented trip to Algiers in 1914. Fauset helped shape how Black life appeared in literature and print. Waring helped shape how Black people appeared through portraiture.
  • Connection back to Marian Anderson at Celestine. Waring painted Marian Anderson, whom we encountered, or will encounter again, at Celestine. Literature, visual art, and music are connected across the cemetery.

Additional detail: Arthur Huff Fauset. Jessie’s younger half-brother, Arthur Huff Fauset, is also buried at Olive. Arthur Fauset died on September 2, 1983. Direct Eden burial. Like Jessie, he was buried directly at Eden decades after the 1923 transfer. Arthur was an educator, civil-rights activist, anthropologist, and folklorist. His book Black Gods of the Metropolis examined Black religious communities in Philadelphia. The siblings provide two related forms of cultural documentation: Jessie studied and shaped Black life through literature and editing; Arthur studied Black life through folklore, anthropology, religion, and education.

Optional deep dive: religion and the Fauset family. [Use if the group has expressed interest in religion.] Jessie and Arthur were children of Redmon Fauset, an African Methodist Episcopal minister. But shared family origins did not mean identical beliefs. Jessie described a strongly religious upbringing. Arthur later became critical of organized religion even while studying Black religious traditions. A religious family can transmit a common language and institutional background without producing identical adult beliefs. Arthur’s research preserved information about religious communities that larger academic institutions often ignored.

Optional deep dive: from reform organizations to cultural institutions. [Use if time remains.] Whipper and Fauset lived in different centuries, but both depended on Black-controlled or Black-oriented institutions. Whipper worked through abolitionist societies, moral-reform organizations, convention networks, businesses, and Underground Railroad relationships. Fauset worked through schools, the NAACP, The Crisis, literary networks, and Pan-African organizing. The specific institutions changed, but the underlying practice continued: Black people created organizations through which they could circulate resources, knowledge, art, and political ideas.

Optional audience questions. “What changes when a displaced cemetery’s name is retained, even though its original land is gone?” “Do you think Whipper and Fauset belong to the same historical story, even though they died eighty-five years apart?”

Olive summary. [Return attention to the section as a whole.] Olive contains two overlapping histories. The first is displacement. William Whipper and others were removed from an African American cemetery in Philadelphia and brought here in 1923. The second is continuity. Jessie and Arthur Fauset were buried directly at Eden after Olive became an established section. Whipper connects Olive backward to Forten, Smith, abolition, business, and the Colored Conventions. Jessie and Arthur Fauset connect it forward to literature, education, folklore, religion, visual art, and the Harlem Renaissance.

The name of a lost cemetery became the name of a new community of the dead.

Transition from Olive, if returning to Celestine. “We began with the struggle to complete Eden’s first burial. We will now return to Celestine to see what Eden became after that first confrontation: a chosen resting place for later generations of Black artists, physicians, religious leaders, and preservers of history.”

If Olive is the final plot. “Olive brings together people who were forced to move after death and people who later chose, or whose families chose, to make Eden their resting place. That combination of displacement and continuity defines Eden as a whole.”

08Closing: Celestine revisited

The return. We began here with a woman whose burial was resisted in 1902. We return to find generations of Black professionals, artists, ministers, and historians who subsequently made Eden their place of rest. All four people below were later burials, not people transferred when Eden opened. That is the bridge into the closing discussion of present-day stewardship and contested reinterment.
