Historic Eden Cemetery

Brand Narrative and Awareness Strategy

Brand narrative, positioning, and the core story of a Black institution built to preserve dignity, memory, and permanence.
PREPARED FOR 1838 BLACK METROPOLIS × HISTORIC EDEN CEMETERY  ·  2026
North Star

Eden is the full-circle answer to a long history of Black dispossession: a place created so that dignity would not end at death, and where the lives of Black Philadelphia could remain visible, protected, and connected to future generations.

Core Positioning

Historic Eden Cemetery is not simply a burial ground. It is a sacred Black cultural landscape created through collective agency at a moment when racism, segregation, urban expansion, condemnation, and the closure of earlier Black cemeteries threatened both the dignity of the dead and the continuity of Black memory. Eden became the community's answer: Black-controlled ground where people could be buried with respect and where their histories would endure.

Today, Eden holds more than 93,000 lives across 17,000 lots on 53 acres — including abolitionists, educators, ministers, veterans, artists, physicians, architects, civic leaders, families, and everyday Black citizens whose collective stories helped build Black Philadelphia and shape the nation. Eden's cultural importance is therefore not based only on who is famous enough to be called "notable." Its power lies in the extraordinary and the everyday resting together, preserving the full social fabric of Black life.

That makes Eden a "full-circle" institution: Black communities created sacred space when dominant systems denied them dignity; Eden later received people displaced from Black burial grounds that had been erased or forced to close; and today the cemetery can return those lives to public memory through preservation, education, research, visitation, and community stewardship.

Brand Promise

Eden protects more than graves. It protects the dignity, evidence, memory, and continuity of Black life — so that what was pushed out of sight is never pushed out of history.

Primary Brand Idea

From displacement to dignity. From burial to memory. From the ancestors to us.

Strategic Objective

Drive awareness of Eden as an essential Black cultural institution and convert that awareness into visitation, partnership, sponsorship, preservation support, and long-term financial sustainability.

Voice, Narrative Architecture & Headlines

How Eden Should Sound

A voice grounded in reverence, agency, intimacy, and historical consequence — never morbidity, nostalgia, or generic heritage tourism.

Brand Voice

  1. Sacred, not solemn-only: Death is present, but the language should center on continuity, dignity, reverence, kinship, and ancestral relationship rather than fear or morbidity.Focus on the spiritual interconnectedness of Blackness.
  2. Historically grounded: Use evidence, names, places, dates, and institutional history to make Eden's significance undeniable.
  3. Black-centered and self-determined: Eden should be described as something Black Philadelphians built, protected, and sustained — not merely as a site where Black people happen to be buried.
  4. Accessible, not flattened: The story should be clear enough for families, students, tourists, and media while retaining intellectual depth.A concise elevator pitch — see Appendix A.
  5. Present-tense: Eden is not a relic. It is an active cemetery, research resource, gathering place, classroom, sacred landscape, and living institution.

Narrative Pillars

  1. Dignity as Resistance — Eden was founded so death would not become another site of racial dispossession. The very existence of Black-controlled burial space is an act of agency.
  2. The Collection Cemetery — When earlier Black burial grounds were forced to close, Eden became a place of return, preservation, and continuity. It gathered what the city displaced.
  3. Black Philadelphia in Full — Eden holds the famous and the familiar. Its 93,000 stories reveal Black life across class, profession, faith, family, politics, art, science, military service, and everyday community.
  4. Memory Across Generations — Eden is where descendants can find evidence of themselves. The cemetery makes ancestry tangible and gives younger generations a place to encounter the people who came before them.
  5. A Living Future — Preservation is not backward-looking. Protecting Eden enables future education, research, public history, family connection, and community ownership.

Proof Points to Repeat Consistently

  • Founded in 1902 amid a Black burial crisis created by segregation, condemnation, urban expansion, and the closure of earlier Black cemeteries.
  • 53 acres of Black-controlled sacred ground in Collingdale, Pennsylvania.
  • More than 93,000 people are entrusted to Eden's care.
  • A "collection cemetery" that received people reinterred from displaced Black burial grounds, including Lebanon and Olive.
  • Lives at Eden span from the eighteenth century to the present, making the cemetery a multigenerational archive of Black life.
  • National Register of Historic Places and National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom designations.
  • Home to figures including Marian Anderson, Octavius V. Catto, William Still, Frances E. W. Harper, Rebecca J. Cole, Julian Abele, James Forten, Pauline Oberdorfer Minor, and Charles L. Workstream 1Awareness

Build Awareness Before Building Social

Near-term priority: make Eden easier to understand, easier to visit, easier to support, and easier to partner with.

1. Website: Make Eden's Significance Immediately Legible

  • The website should function as Eden's primary narrative hub and conversion tool. Before expanding social media, every visitor, reporter, funder, descendant, educator, and partner should be able to understand Eden's story within seconds and know exactly what action to take next.
  • Lead the homepage with the core positioning: Eden as a sacred Black cultural landscape created to preserve dignity and memory.
  • Build clear pathways for Visit, Find an Ancestor / Grounds Map, Learn the History, Notable Residents, Tours & Education, and Support Eden.
  • Use the "From the Ordinary to the Extraordinary" framework to prevent the site from becoming only a celebrity cemetery.
  • Make the Beginnings story prominent: Lebanon and Olive, the burial crisis, the blocked gates, and the first burial demonstrate why Eden exists.
  • Pair historical interpretation with visible donation and sponsorship opportunities throughout the site — not only on a single Donate page.
  • Create a concise "Why Eden Matters" page that partners and media can easily cite and share.
Success Looks Like

More people understand why Eden matters; more Black Philadelphians see Eden as part of their cultural inheritance; more institutions recognize a credible role for partnership; and awareness translates into the financial support needed to protect the cemetery for the next generation.

2. Community Partnerships: Restore Eden to the Cultural Map

Community engagement should make Eden feel less like a place people encounter only through death and more like a place Black communities can visit, learn, gather, and commune with memory.

  • Develop recurring heritage days, family-history sessions, women's history programming, veteran remembrance, and thematic walking tours.
  • Partner with Black churches, Divine Nine organizations, schools, HBCU alumni networks, neighborhood associations, genealogical groups, and Black cultural institutions.
  • Use the phrase "That's ours" as a community ownership frame: Eden is a Black institution worth visiting, protecting, and claiming.
  • Reintroduce traditions of cemetery visitation as reflection, learning, and ancestral connection — while respecting active burial operations and sacred space.
  • Create community ambassador opportunities so trusted partners can bring their own audiences into Eden.
  • For a step-by-step approach to starting and testing a partnership, see Appendix C.

Workstream 2Sponsorship & Financial Sustainability

Turn Cultural Importance Into Sustainable Support

Because preservation without resources is vulnerability. Sponsorship should be a primary brand activation, not an afterthought.

1. Sponsorship Positioning

Eden should not ask sponsors merely to "support a cemetery." The case for support is larger: sponsors are helping protect one of the most important repositories of Black Philadelphia's cultural memory, preserve 93,000 individual histories, expand public access to a nationally significant landscape, and ensure that Black-controlled sacred space survives for future generations.

Core Sponsorship Message

Preserving Eden means preserving evidence: names, families, institutions, stories, monuments, burial records, and the physical ground that connects Black Philadelphia's past to its future.

2. Priority Sponsorship Platforms

Preservation Partner: Support monument conservation, landscape care, archival protection, and preservation of historic sections.

Digital Memory Partner: Fund digitization of records, interactive mapping, searchable family-history resources, and interpretive storytelling.

Education Partner: Underwrite school visits, educator resources, student transportation, tours, and public-history programming.

Community Legacy Partner: Support free community days, genealogy events, women's programs, veteran remembrance, and intergenerational gatherings.

3. Who to Approach

  • Philadelphia-region corporations with commitments to Black communities, historic preservation, education, or civic engagement.
  • Foundations focused on African American history, archives, preservation, public humanities, genealogy, and place-based community development.
  • Healthcare, architecture, higher education, arts, faith, and financial institutions whose own histories intersect with people buried at Eden.

4. Immediate 90-Day Brand Actions

  • Finalize the brand narrative and "Why Eden Matters" language so every pitch uses one consistent story.
  • Create a sponsorship one-sheet/deck with 4–5 fundable platforms and clear impact language.
  • Revise the website homepage and support pathways to convert awareness into donations, sponsorship inquiries, tour bookings, and partnerships.
  • Identify 20–30 priority institutional prospects and match each prospect to a specific sponsorship platform rather than sending a generic ask.
  • Defer broad social-media expansion until Eden has the capacity to sustain it well.

Appendix A
Elevator Pitches & Statement of Importance
Drafted from workshop voices, community input, and institutional history — June 2026

Why Eden Is Important — A Foundational Paragraph

Eden Cemetery is not simply a burial ground — it is the accumulated memory of Black America's most audacious act of self-determination.

Since Black Philadelphians began establishing their own sacred spaces in the 1790s — churches, beneficial societies, and cemeteries that offered dignity where the dominant culture offered none — they were building toward Eden.

Established in 1902 by Black founders who refused to accept that death would be another site of dispossession, Eden became the guardian of what other institutions could not hold. When Lebanon Cemetery and Olive Cemetery — home to Philadelphia's Black citizens — were forced to close and their residents reinterred, Eden absorbed them all.

What this means is that Eden now holds the complete record of Black Philadelphia's founding generation: the abolitionists who operated the Underground Railroad in plain sight, the ministers who built the first Black churches, the physicians who founded the first African American graduate medical programs, the poets who told America it could not be free while it held people in chains, the architects who designed the buildings of elite American institutions, and the musicians who carried the sorrow and joy of a people to the world's great stages.

Philadelphia was the civic laboratory of Black America — the city where Black people invented how to protest, how to claim rights, and how to build institutions that could sustain a community under conditions of profound oppression — and the people who did that are buried at Eden.

When you walk Eden's 53 acres, you are not visiting a cemetery. You are time-traveling through the foundation of Black American identity, standing on ground that holds 93,000 ordinary people who did extraordinary things — in a city that taught the world what freedom really means.

Ten Elevator Pitches

Each pitch offers a distinct angle. Phrases in italics are lifted directly from workshop participants.

01The Founding Pitch

Philadelphia was the civic laboratory of Black America. The first HBCU. The first Black churches. The first beneficial societies. The first abolitionist networks that put the cracks in the foundation of American slavery. The men and women who built those institutions — who taught the world how to protest, how to claim civil rights, how to build a nation within a nation — are buried at Eden Cemetery. You cannot do Black Philly history without Eden Cemetery. And you cannot understand America without Black Philly.

02The F.U.B.U. Pitch

In 1902, a group of Black Philadelphians did something radical: they built their own. For generations, Black people had been denied dignity in death — buried in unmarked graves, in segregated sections, in cemeteries they did not control. So Black Philadelphia built Eden. For You. By You. Ninety-three thousand souls rest on 53 acres of Black-owned, Black-controlled, sacred ground. That's ours. It has been ours from the beginning. It still is.

03The Underground Railroad Pitch

William Still — the father of the Underground Railroad, the man who documented every freedom seeker he helped escape, who kept the records that became the foundation of African American genealogy — is buried at Eden. He is one of 93,000. Eden is a designated National Underground Railroad Network to Freedom site and is listed on the National Register of Historic Places. The freedom movement didn't end with emancipation. The proof is right here — seeing what is no longer, and what must never be forgotten.

04The "Hi Oprah" Pitch (lifted directly from workshop)

"Hi Oprah. A serious historical injustice needs to be rectified. Eden Cemetery's significance has been strategically undermined — intentionally — and it has caused broken trust and lost community connections to Black American history, from the ordinary to the extraordinary, for over 124 years." Eden is where Stedman Graham's family rests. It is where Black Philadelphia rests. It needs a champion. We're asking you to be one.

05The Time-Travel Pitch

What if you could sit in the same space as Marian Anderson, Frances Watkins Harper, and William Still? Eden Cemetery is time travel — the past in the present. It is where 93,000 stories of ordinary people who did extraordinary things are waiting to be heard. Stories = History. Past = Now. The result of what was is the continuation of what we are. Come sit with your ancestors. History ignored is history repeated. Eden won't let you forget.

06The Civic Democracy Pitch

The Declaration of Independence was signed in Philadelphia. But it was Black Philadelphians who forced America to live up to it — who challenged its hypocrisy from the very beginning, in their churches, their courts, their newspapers, and their streets. What they practiced in Philadelphia, the world eventually practiced. Philly is at the center of a national conversation about how American history should be remembered. The architects of that tradition — from the ordinary to the extraordinary, for over 124 years — are buried at Eden. This is not a cemetery. It is the birthplace of American democracy's conscience.

07The Sacred Space Pitch

Since the 1790s, Black Philadelphians have protected their right to honor their dead — in dignity, in community, in reverence. In a time when enslaved people had no control over their bodies in life or in death, having a headstone, having a grave someone could visit, having ancestors who had come to a place of rest — that was resistance. That was love. Eden is the culmination of over 200 years of that tradition. A safe space. A sacred space. A third space. Come honor them. Come sit in the beauty. Come see what is no longer — and feel what endures.

08The Reinterment / Justice Pitch

Lebanon Cemetery. Olive Cemetery. The great Black burial grounds of Philadelphia — home to some of the most important Black Americans in history — were forced to close. Forced. Their residents were reinterred at Eden. Other cemeteries were not forced to move. Only theirs. Even in death, the mistreatment continued. But Eden held them. Eden holds them still. What looks like a cemetery is actually an act of resistance — 124 years of refusing to let Black history be buried twice.

09The America's-Oldest-Black-Cemetery Pitch

Eden Cemetery is a representation of the importance of the identity of Black life — and one of the oldest continuously operating Black-founded cemeteries in the United States. Its 93,000 residents include an Olympic gold medalist, the composer of gospel's greatest hymns, a U.S. Congressman, the architect of elite American universities, the abolitionist who documented the Underground Railroad, and one of America's greatest singers. This is not a local story. A representation of Black history — for half of America's history — has persisted here. Eden is America.

10The Future Pitch

We need Eden not just because of what happened in the past — but because of what is happening right now. Black spaces need support now more than ever. Eden is how we transport the past to our future. It is the place where we learn from the people who built Black America — who practiced Afrofuturism before the word existed, who imagined a future they might not live to see and built it anyway. We need resources to continue informing future generations about our history. We need Eden to survive — so the stories it holds can teach us ways to improve our now.
